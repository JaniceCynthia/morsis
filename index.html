Zone A · Sky

The air is full of syntax.

Birds string calls together with rules. Ravens recruit allies to food with a dedicated yell. Starlings never stop learning new sounds.

Zone B · Land

Some messages travel through your feet.

Elephant rumbles sit below human hearing and ripple through the ground for kilometres. Prairie dogs pack the colour, size and speed of an intruder into one alarm call.

Zone C · Sea

Down here, sound travels four times faster.

Water carries voices across ocean basins. Humpback songs spread between populations like culture. Dolphins answer to the whistle that works as their name.

Inside the FT-1 field transmitter.

Four layers turn a sentence into a signal an animal can parse, and a call back into words.

  1. 01 Listen

    Hydrophones and mic arrays

    A six-capsule array and a ferrite loop antenna capture 1 Hz to 150 kHz, so infrasonic rumbles and dolphin clicks land in one recording, tagged with GPS, depth and time.

  2. 02 Segment

    Find the units

    The DSP-3 board splits the signal into units: clicks, whistles, moans, chirps. Its shielded front end strips boat, wind and surf noise before anything is interpreted.

  3. 03 Map meaning

    Align sound with context

    The Decode-3 co-processor links each unit to what was happening: who was nearby, what they did next, where the food or the danger was. Every hypothesis goes to a sealed field log.

  4. 04 Emit

    Answer in their signal

    Your sentence is composed from units the species already uses, then played through the wideband driver at the right band and level, on cell or crank power, and only under research permits and welfare review.

Say something. Something answers.

Pick a species, type a sentence, transmit. Your words are encoded into that species’ own call units. Decode-3 translates the reply, with a confidence score and the field note behind it.

    We didn’t invent animal language. We’re learning to read it.

    Morsis builds on decades of peer-reviewed field research. These are some of the findings our models start from.

    Elephants call each other by name-like calls.
    Wild African elephants responded more strongly to playbacks of calls originally addressed to them than to calls addressed to others.Pardo et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2024
    Sperm whale codas have a combinatorial structure.
    Click patterns vary in rhythm, tempo and ornamentation, which combine into a far larger repertoire than once thought.Sharma et al., Nature Communications, 2024
    Prairie dog alarm calls describe the intruder.
    Gunnison’s prairie dogs produce different calls for different predators, and even for the colour of a human’s clothing.Slobodchikoff et al., Animal Cognition, 2009
    Dolphins answer to their own name.
    Bottlenose dolphins responded when they heard copies of their own signature whistle, and rarely to others.King & Janik, PNAS, 2013
    In songbirds, call order changes meaning.
    Japanese tits respond to an alert-then-recruit sequence, but not to the same calls played in reverse order.Suzuki et al., Nature Communications, 2016
    Humpback songs travel between populations.
    New song types spread eastward across the South Pacific, from population to population, over a few years.Garland et al., Current Biology, 2011

    Identity

    One mark, wherever the work goes.

    The same mark, palette and type go on every badge, notebook, screen and post the lab puts out. If it carries this identity, it came from Morsis. If it doesn’t, it didn’t.

    • Every researcher carries it.

      Field staff wear a Morsis ID and lab coat at every station, so you always know who is recording.

    • Every record carries it.

      Field notes, logs and published findings are issued under one mark, one palette and one typeface.

    • Only one voice speaks for us.

      We post as morsis.lab and write from lab@morsis.org. Anything else isn’t Morsis.
