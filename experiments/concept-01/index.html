01 Method

Translation isn't about words.

Animals don't share our vocabulary, but they share our physics. Alarm sounds fast. Calm sounds slow. Curiosity rises at the end. Morsis models the intent inside human speech and re-renders it in the acoustic grammar each species evolved to hear.

01 / Listen

Decompose speech into intent

We strip a sentence down to its prosody (pitch contour, tempo, emphasis) and classify what it means: greeting, alarm, query, affiliation, food.

02 / Map

Find the species' grammar

Each intent is mapped onto signal structures learned from field recordings: a humpback's song unit, a dog's rising whine, a wren's trill.

03 / Transmit

Synthesize, then verify

The signal is rendered inside the species' hearing range. We then measure behavioural response, because a translation only counts if it's understood.

Built on motivation-structural rules first described by ornithologist Eugene Morton (1977): across birds and mammals, harsh low sounds tend to signal hostility, while tonal high sounds tend to signal appeasement.

03 Field notes

Three listeners, three grammars.

Humpback whale

Megaptera novaeangliae

Signal
Song units, phrases, themes
Model
v0.4 · in study

Humpback song has structure: units build phrases, and phrases build themes. That gives us a grammar we can write into.

Domestic dog

Canis familiaris

Signal
Bark rate, whine contour
Model
v1.2 · field trials

Dogs read pitch contour and tempo better than any word. A rising, tonal call reads as an invitation, and fast, harsh pulses read as a warning.

Eurasian wren

Troglodytes troglodytes

Signal
Trills, frequency sweeps
Model
v0.2 · early

A wren trills faster than human ears can separate, so to us it sounds like a blur. Our models are built to hear each note.
