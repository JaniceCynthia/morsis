Morsis is a research lab translating human speech into signals animals can understand, and their signals back into ours. We record, decode and, under strict protocols, answer.

Every species already speaks. Whales sing in structured songs, elephants call each other by name, prairie dogs describe the predator they see. We build the models that sit between us.

02 · How it works

Inside the FT-1 field transmitter.

Four layers turn a sentence into a signal an animal can parse, and a call back into words. Scroll to take it apart.

Drag to rotate
  1. The instrument

    Built to be taken apart.

    Keep scrolling. The FT-1 assembles, then opens up one layer at a time.

  2. 01 · Listen

    Hydrophones and mic arrays

    Six-channel capture from 1 Hz to 150 kHz, so infrasonic rumbles and dolphin clicks land in the same recording, tagged with GPS, depth and time.

  3. 02 · Segment

    Find the units

    The DSP board splits the signal into units: clicks, whistles, moans, chirps. Noise from boats, wind and surf is stripped out before anything is interpreted.

  4. 03 · Map meaning

    Align sound with context

    Decode-3 links each unit to what was happening: who was nearby, what they did next, where the food or the danger was. Meaning is inferred, never assumed.

  5. 04 · Emit

    Answer in their signal

    Your sentence is composed from units the species already uses, then played back at the right band and level, only under research permits and welfare review.

  6. Seven layers

    One instrument, every part accounted for.

    Housing, battery, DSP board, Decode-3 core, transducers, signal scale and control face. Drag to turn it.

03 · Open a channel

Say something. Something answers.

Pick a species, type a sentence, press transmit.

  1. Your words are encoded into that species’ own call units. You hear and see the signal.
  2. The animal’s reply comes back as raw signal on the spectrogram.
  3. Decode-3 translates it, with a confidence score and the field note behind it.

Elephant rumbles are too low for human ears. Use the hearing shift dial to move any call up or down by octaves.

04 · What science already hears

We didn’t invent animal language. We’re learning to read it.

Morsis builds on decades of peer-reviewed field research. These are some of the findings our models start from.

Elephants

They call each other by name-like calls.

Wild African elephants responded more strongly to playbacks of calls originally addressed to them than to calls addressed to others.

Pardo et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2024
Sperm whales

Their codas have a combinatorial structure.

Click patterns vary in rhythm, tempo and ornamentation, which combine into a far larger repertoire than once thought.

Sharma et al., Nature Communications, 2024
Prairie dogs

Alarm calls describe the intruder.

Gunnison’s prairie dogs produce different calls for different predators, and even for the colour of a human’s clothing.

Slobodchikoff et al., Animal Cognition, 2009
Dolphins

They answer to their own name.

Bottlenose dolphins responded when they heard copies of their own signature whistle, and rarely to others.

King & Janik, PNAS, 2013
Songbirds

Call order changes meaning.

Japanese tits respond to an alert-then-recruit sequence, but not to the same calls played in reverse order.

Suzuki et al., Nature Communications, 2016
Humpbacks

Songs travel between populations.

New song types spread eastward across the South Pacific, from population to population, over a few years.

Garland et al., Current Biology, 2011
